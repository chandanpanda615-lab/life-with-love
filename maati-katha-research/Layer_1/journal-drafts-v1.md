# Journal Drafts v1 — Maati Katha

*Five draft journal entries written in the Maati Katha editorial voice. Built from Layer 1 spatial research, demographic and linguistic findings, and the observation pool from the first round of photo descriptions. These are first drafts. Read them in a few days with fresh eyes. Edit for what feels true and what feels off. Anything that survives that pass is real material for the website.*

*Editorial register: slow, observational, present-tense, undramatic, no travel-brochure language. The voice of a person from Sarangada writing to a foreign visitor who has not yet arrived.*

---

## Entry 1 — The Day Flows West

A village has a direction. Sarangada's direction is west.

The sun rises over the fields north and east of us — ordered ground, divided by earthen bunds, planted with paddy in monsoon and turmeric in the dry months. By midday it sits high over the market spine, the one-kilometre stretch of road that holds the lower market and the upper market and most of the people in between. By evening it has crossed over the houses, over the courtyards, over the chulha smoke rising out of cooking fires, and it sets behind the hills to the west — the jungle side, the side that is still mostly forest.

Every day in Sarangada moves from cultivation into wilderness, from the social into the elemental. You wake to the fields. You sleep facing the trees. We have not designed this, and we cannot change it. It is just where the village happens to be on the earth, and which way the earth happens to turn. But it is also the shape of the seven days you spend with us. You arrive from the south. You learn the eastern rhythms first. By Day Six you are walking west.

## Entry 2 — On the Word Maati

In Hindi, in Bengali, in Odia, *maati* means soil. But that translation is not the whole of it.

*Maati* also means homeland — your soil, the ground you came from. *Maati ka rishta* is a relationship of soil, which is a relationship that has nothing to do with paperwork or politics. It is the kind of relationship a farmer has with one specific field after thirty seasons. It is the kind of relationship a returning son has with the road he walked as a child.

We named this project *Maati Katha* because the soil here has a story. Not metaphorically. Literally. The red lateritic earth of Kandhamal is what makes our turmeric the colour it is. The fine clay underneath is what holds the monsoon water for the paddy. The pediment slope at the base of these hills is the reason a village settled exactly here, and not three kilometres east where the floods would reach. Every act of life in Sarangada sits on top of millions of years of geological decision.

The *katha* is what the soil tells you, if you stay long enough to listen.

## Entry 3 — A Mixed Village

I should be honest with you about what kind of village this is. Sarangada is not a tribal village. It is also not a non-tribal village. It is a mixed village, the way most villages in Kandhamal actually are.

We have Kondh families, who are the indigenous people of this district and who have been here longer than any document can record. We have Brahmin families and Kshatriya families and other communities whose ancestors came from elsewhere and settled. We have Christian families, converts of one or two generations, whose churches sit alongside the older shrines. The languages spoken in Sarangada follow the same layered logic — Odia in the market and the school, Kui in the older Kondh households, Hindi increasingly understood by the younger ones because of Instagram and YouTube reels.

If you came expecting a single culture performed for you, you will leave disappointed. What we offer instead is something rarer and harder to package — a real village, with all of its layers, sharing one small piece of red soil. The history of these layers is not always peaceful. Kandhamal has seen its difficult years, and we will not pretend otherwise. But for the seven days you are here, you will see what coexistence actually looks like at the level of daily life — the same well, the same road, the same monsoon arriving on everyone at once.

## Entry 4 — On Coexistence, Material

A doorway here is mud-plastered earth holding up a corrugated metal roof.

A field is shaped by a wooden tool that has not changed in three hundred years and watered by a borewell that runs on electricity that arrived in this village within living memory.

A meal is cooked on a chulha fired with wood gathered from the forest and served on a leaf-plate that grew there too, and afterwards someone takes a selfie with it.

This is not contradiction. This is just what a working village looks like in 2026. The thatched-roof hut and the asbestos-sheet roof are next to each other because both kept the rain out and one cost less. The man fishing with a cantilevered lift net at the edge of the river is also the man you might pass an hour later on a motorcycle. We do not stage authenticity by removing the modern. The modern is also part of what is true here.

When you visit, do not photograph only the old. The power lines crossing the field at sunset are part of the picture. So is the smartphone in the farmer's pocket. So is the school where the next generation is learning to leave, or to stay, or to leave and come back differently.

## Entry 5 — What to Expect on the First Morning

You will wake before you are ready. Not because we will wake you, but because village mornings are not quiet the way city mornings are not quiet — there will be roosters, there will be voices, there will be the specific sound of a chulha being lit in a courtyard somewhere on the same road as you.

You will probably step outside before you have had tea. The light at that hour, the way it falls across the eastern fields and along the market spine, is one of the things you came for, even if you didn't know it. The air will smell of woodsmoke and damp earth and, depending on the season, drying turmeric. The sky in May is white and waiting. The sky in August is heavy and moving. The sky in November is clean enough to show you stars at five in the morning.

Someone will hand you tea. The tea will be too sweet by your standards, and you will drink it anyway, because the alternative is to make a small social statement on the first morning, and you will not want to.

This is the seven days beginning. Not the rituals, not the work in the fields, not the jungle walk on Day Six. This. Standing barefoot on a mud-plastered veranda at six in the morning, holding a small glass of sweet tea, with a village waking up around you that is not yours and is also, for one week, exactly yours.

When reading these drafts again, mark in the margin:

- **TRUE** — sentences that match what Sarangada actually is and feel right in your voice
- **OFF** — sentences where the AI assist showed up and the voice slipped
- **WRONG** — anything factually incorrect about the village
- **MINE** — replace with something only you would write

Anything left untouched after that pass is real Maati Katha material. Anything marked WRONG must be fixed before the website goes anywhere near a public audience.
