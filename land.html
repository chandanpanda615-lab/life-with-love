The land

Sarangada sits where it sits because of the soil. These are the notes, the plain description, and the photographs as they get collected.

notes from the village
What maati means

Maati means soil. It also means the place you are from.

The soil here is red, full of iron. That is why our turmeric comes out the colour it does. Under the red there is clay, and the clay holds monsoon water long enough for paddy to grow.

The village sits exactly where it sits because of that soil. Three kilometres east and the floods would reach it.

Katha means story. The soil has one. You have to stay a while to hear it.

A mixed village

Sarangada is not a tribal village. It is not a non-tribal village either. It is mixed, the way most villages in Kandhamal actually are.

Kondh families, who were here first. Brahmin and Kshatriya families who came later. Christian families whose churches stand near the older shrines.

Kandhamal has had hard years. We will not pretend otherwise. But the well is the same well, the road is the same road, and the monsoon arrives on everyone at once.

Old and new, side by side

A mud wall holding up a metal roof. A wooden plough next to an electric borewell. Food cooked on a chulha, served on a leaf plate, photographed on a phone.

That is not a contradiction. That is what a working village looks like in 2026. Both roofs kept the rain out. One cost less.

So when you come, do not photograph only the old parts. The power lines over the field at sunset are in the picture too.

The first morning

You will wake up before you are ready. Roosters, voices, someone lighting a chulha down the road.

You will step outside before you have had tea. The air smells of woodsmoke and wet earth, and in the right season, drying turmeric.

Then someone hands you tea. It is too sweet. You drink it anyway.

what the land says

Bright green paddy fields terraced into the hillside, their earthen bunds holding water.

A single-story building with mud-plastered walls and simple wooden doors. A pair of sandals lies on the bare dirt ground outside.

The atmosphere appears humid and still.

Fire-roasted vegetables — charred potatoes, red onions, tomatoes, a green chili — on a piece of aluminum foil.

A communal meal eaten directly on the bare reddish-brown earth.

Different eras of technology and material culture coexist.

The rust, the bare earth, and the worn building are presented plainly.

A week of unscripted life beneath the mist of the Eastern Ghats.

The warmth of shared meals on leaf-plates and bare red soil.

Seven days that leave you part of Sarangada's quiet story.
