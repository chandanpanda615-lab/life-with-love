on the word maati

In Hindi, in Bengali, in Odia, maati means soil. But that translation is not the whole of it.

Maati also means homeland — your soil, the ground you came from. Maati ka rishta is a relationship of soil, which is a relationship that has nothing to do with paperwork or politics. It is the kind of relationship a farmer has with one specific field after thirty seasons. It is the kind of relationship a returning son has with the road he walked as a child.

We named this project Maati Katha because the soil here has a story. Not metaphorically. Literally. The red lateritic earth of Kandhamal is what makes our turmeric the colour it is. The fine clay underneath is what holds the monsoon water for the paddy. The pediment slope at the base of these hills is the reason a village settled exactly here, and not three kilometres east where the floods would reach.

The katha is what the soil tells you, if you stay long enough to listen.

The deep red, lateritic soil forms the unpaved road and the riverbanks.

the day flows west

A village has a direction. Sarangada's direction is west.

The sun rises over the fields north and east of us — ordered ground, divided by earthen bunds, planted with paddy in monsoon and turmeric in the dry months. By midday it sits high over the market spine, the one-kilometre stretch of road that holds most of the people. By evening it has crossed over the houses, over the courtyards, over the chulha smoke rising out of cooking fires, and it sets behind the hills to the west — the jungle side, the side that is still mostly forest.

Every day in Sarangada moves from cultivation into wilderness, from the social into the elemental. You wake to the fields. You sleep facing the trees. It is also the shape of the seven days you spend with us. You arrive from the south. You learn the eastern rhythms first. By Day Six you are walking west.

a mixed village

I should be honest with you about what kind of village this is. Sarangada is not a tribal village. It is also not a non-tribal village. It is a mixed village, the way most villages in Kandhamal actually are.

We have Kondh families, who are the indigenous people of this district and who have been here longer than any document can record. We have Brahmin and Kshatriya families whose ancestors came from elsewhere and settled. We have Christian families whose churches sit alongside the older shrines. The languages follow the same layered logic — Odia in the market and the school, Kui in the older Kondh households, Hindi increasingly understood by the younger ones because of reels.

If you came expecting a single culture performed for you, you will leave disappointed. What we offer instead is rarer and harder to package — a real village, with all of its layers, sharing one small piece of red soil. The history of these layers is not always peaceful. Kandhamal has seen its difficult years, and we will not pretend otherwise. But for the seven days you are here, you will see what coexistence actually looks like at the level of daily life — the same well, the same road, the same monsoon arriving on everyone at once.

The peaks of the distant hills are shrouded in low-hanging mist.

on coexistence, material

A doorway here is mud-plastered earth holding up a corrugated metal roof. A field is shaped by a wooden tool that has not changed in three hundred years and watered by a borewell that runs on electricity that arrived within living memory. A meal is cooked on a chulha fired with wood from the forest and served on a leaf-plate that grew there too, and afterwards someone takes a selfie with it.

This is not contradiction. This is just what a working village looks like in 2026. The thatched-roof hut and the asbestos-sheet roof are next to each other because both kept the rain out and one cost less. We do not stage authenticity by removing the modern. The modern is also part of what is true here.

When you visit, do not photograph only the old.

The power lines crossing the field at sunset are part of the picture. So is the smartphone in the farmer's pocket. So is the school where the next generation is learning to leave, or to stay, or to leave and come back differently.

The warm, low-angled light of sunrise, the sun a sliver of orange on the horizon.

the first morning

You will wake before you are ready. Not because we will wake you, but because village mornings are not quiet the way city mornings are not quiet — there will be roosters, there will be voices, there will be the specific sound of a chulha being lit in a courtyard somewhere on the same road as you.

You will probably step outside before you have had tea. The air will smell of woodsmoke and damp earth and, depending on the season, drying turmeric. The sky in May is white and waiting. The sky in August is heavy and moving. The sky in November is clean enough to show you stars at five in the morning.

Someone will hand you tea. The tea will be too sweet by your standards, and you will drink it anyway, because the alternative is to make a small social statement on the first morning, and you will not want to.

A village waking up around you that is not yours and is also, for one week, exactly yours.

getting here

There is no railway into Kandhamal. Most people fly into Bhubaneswar and drive — roughly 250 kilometres, most of a day, the last stretch climbing west into the Eastern Ghats. The nearest railhead is Berhampur, about 150 kilometres south, with onward options at Bhubaneswar, Sambalpur and Muniguda. However you come, the final approach is by road, from the south, past Baliguda. The drive is not incidental. It is where the plain gives way to forest, and it is the first thing the land tells you.

the village

Sarangada is a mixed village strung along about a kilometre of market road — Kondh, Brahmin, Kshatriya and Christian families sharing the same red soil. Odia in the market and the school, Kui in the older Kondh homes, Hindi among the young. Pincode 762106. 20.227°N, 84.124°E.

what to expect

Plain comforts, honestly. You sleep in the village, not beside it. The tea is too sweet. There is no wifi after seven, and nobody minds. Days begin before you are ready and end facing the trees.

who it's for

Anyone who wants a place as it is rather than as it is performed. If you came for a single culture staged for a camera, you will leave disappointed. If you came to be absorbed for seven days, this is that.

Seven days. One village. Not open yet.

This is a pilot in design. The route, the host model, and what the village is willing to share are still being settled — in the village, before they are settled on a website. Nothing here is bookable, and no date is being promised.
